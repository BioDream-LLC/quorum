# Casebook taxonomy: the categories, from the standards rather than from experience

This document exists because the categories in this project were being discovered one accident at a time.

`pump.cb` could not respond to a single symptom, and that was found by comparing against an alarm system — after which
the missing idea got the invented name "condition". `tank-409` decided on the absence of objections, and that was found
by a reachability check — after which the missing idea got the invented name "relies on absence". Both are established
concepts with standard names, defined semantics, and in one case a burden-of-proof theory attached.

Inventing vocabulary that already exists is not just inelegant. It means the **rest** of the taxonomy is unavailable: the
categories you have not yet stumbled into stay invisible. So the lists below come from the relevant standards and the
argumentation literature, with each entry mapped to a quorum construct and marked with whether `cbc` can check it.

The honest caveat first: **no source claims completeness.** [Schemes, Critical Questions, and Complete Argument
Evaluation](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10503-020-09512-4) states plainly that the argumentation field "currently
lacks a method for providing a complete argument evaluation". These are the best available enumerations, not a proof that
nothing is missing. They are still far better than accumulating categories by collision.

## 1. What a claim can BE — the evidence hierarchy

From **ISO 13379-1** (*Condition monitoring and diagnostics of machines — data interpretation and diagnostics
techniques*), whose Failure Mode Symptoms Analysis is the standard method for deciding what to monitor in order to detect
a given failure mode, and **ISO 14224** (*collection and exchange of reliability and maintenance data*), which splits
every failure into mode, cause and mechanism.

| level | meaning | quorum construct | cbc checks? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **descriptor** | a measured quantity: a number off an instrument | `channel` with `unit`, `range`, `stale_after` | yes — range and staleness are mandatory |
| **symptom** | a descriptor crossing a threshold, i.e. a proposition | `observe`, generated by a `channel` threshold | yes — hysteresis (`release`) is mandatory |
| **failure mode** | the manner in which the item fails to perform | `claim`, usually `needs` two or more symptoms | partially |
| **failure mechanism** | the physical process producing the mode | usually a comment; occasionally a `claim` | no |
| **failure cause** | the circumstance that initiated it | `observe` or `excuse` | no |

**This is the hierarchy that would have prevented the pump defect.** A failure *mode* is an inference from symptoms and
deserves corroboration before anyone acts. But a **symptom can itself be the damaging thing** — a bearing above its
temperature limit is damaging whatever the mechanism, and so is audible cavitation — and in those cases waiting for a
second symptom means waiting for the damage. ISO 13379's FMSA works mode-by-mode and asks which symptoms detect it; it
does not ask the reverse question, *which symptoms warrant action on their own*. That reverse question is the one
`pump.cb` failed.

**Authoring rule.** For every symptom, ask: *is the state this symptom reports damaging by itself?* If yes, it needs a
claim of its own. quorum cannot check this — a "this is damaging" claim and a "this diagnoses X" claim are structurally
identical, both being `claim A needs B`.

## 2. What a defeater can BE — the attack taxonomy

Three attack types from **ASPIC+**, which is the standard translation target and already what quorum implements:

| attack | what it denies | quorum | preference-dependent? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **undermining** | a premise | `doubt ... undermines <obs>` | yes |
| **rebutting** | the conclusion | `X rebuts Y` | yes |
| **undercutting** | the inference itself | `X undercuts Y` | **no** |

Undercutting being preference-independent is not a detail: a more-severe claim cannot out-rank an argument that says the
*reasoning* does not apply. quorum implements this, and `severity` deliberately never cancels an undercut.

### 2a. The part that was missing: assumption vs exception

From the **Carneades** model ([Gordon, Prakken & Walton
2007](https://pure.rug.nl/ws/files/2796516/GordonPrakkenWalton2007a.pdf)), critical questions are modelled as additional
premises of three kinds — *ordinary premises*, *presumptions* (assumptions), and *exceptions* — precisely because they
carry different burdens of proof:

> Assumptions hold, by default, until they have been questioned. With exceptions the negation holds, by default, until
> the exception has been supported by sufficient arguments.
>
> — *Modeling Critical Questions as Additional Premises*, Walton et al. (paraphrased; see also Carneades above)

**This is the formal name for what §7 of the authoring guide argues on instinct.** Restated for casebooks:

| form | behaviour | safe? |
|---|---|---|
| **exception** | the defeater fires only when something is positively asserted | **yes** — silence changes nothing |
| **assumption** | the claim holds until something asserts otherwise | **only with care** |

Both are legitimate and quorum can express both. The `tank-409` defect was an assumption where an exception was needed:
`continue_transfer when inlet_flow` treated the *absence* of an objection as permission. The fix, `needs inlet_flow
level_safe`, made the permission an exception — something must positively assert the level is safe.

And the deliberate use of the assumption form is `condition_unestablished` in `gearbox.cb`: nobody has established the
machine's condition, and that **holds by default** until a positive act defeats it. That is the Piper Alpha pattern, and
it is the correct place for an assumption — the burden of proof is on whoever claims the work was done.

**Authoring rule.** For every claim, ask: *does this hold by default, or only when something asserts it?* If by default,
the burden of proof is on whoever wants to defeat it, and that is a decision to make consciously rather than by accident.

## 3. What a consequence can BE — and why one category needs special handling

From **RCM** (SAE JA1011/JA1012) and mirrored in ISO 14224's failure-consequence coding:

| category | quorum handling | cbc checks? |
|---|---|---|
| **hidden** | the failure is not evident to operators in normal duty | **yes, indirectly** — see below |
| **safety** | it hurts someone | `severity` 8–10, and the `safe` action |
| **environmental** | it breaches a consent | `severity` 8–10 |
| **operational** | it costs production | `severity` 4–7 |
| **non-operational** | it costs only the repair | `severity` 1–3 |

**Hidden failures are the category that most needs this treatment, and quorum already addresses it — by accident of good
design rather than by having read RCM.** A hidden failure is one where *the absence of a symptom is not evidence of
health*: the protective device that would have tripped is itself broken, and nothing says so until it is needed. That is
precisely what `<channel>_unavailable` and `<channel>_flatline` are for, and it is why `cbc` warns when a casebook
reasons about neither — a dead sensor reporting nothing looks exactly like a healthy machine.

CAPECO is a hidden failure: a transmitter frozen at a plausible 45% is not an alarm, it is silence that resembles health.

**Authoring rule.** For every channel, ask: *if this instrument died quietly, what would the casebook conclude?* If the
answer is "that everything is fine", the casebook has a hidden failure and cbc's warning is telling you so.

## 4. The checklist

Not a proof of completeness. A systematic pass, so the next category costs a review rather than an incident.

**Per symptom**
- [ ] Is the state it reports damaging on its own? If so it needs its own claim — a *condition*, not only a diagnosis.
- [ ] Does it have hysteresis? (`release`, which cbc makes mandatory.)
- [ ] Can the instrument reporting it fail quietly? (`stale_after`, `flatline_after`.)

**Per claim**
- [ ] Does it hold by default (**assumption**) or only when asserted (**exception**)? Chosen, not inherited.
- [ ] What would make the conclusion wrong even though the evidence holds? → a **rebuttal**.
- [ ] What would make the *inference* inapplicable? → an **undercut**.
- [ ] What would make the *evidence* untrustworthy? → an **undermine**.
- [ ] Is its consequence hidden, safety, environmental, operational or non-operational? Does `severity` agree?

**Per action**
- [ ] Is there a state where this fires and should not?
- [ ] Is the safe fallback declared rather than assumed? (A drone must keep flying.)

**Per casebook**
- [ ] Does at least one claim have a defeater? (cbc refuses otherwise — a casebook nothing can argue against is a
      decision tree.)
- [ ] Does every claim reach an action? (cbc reports `NO PATH`.)
- [ ] Does anything reason about each `_unavailable` and `_flatline`? (cbc warns.)
- [ ] Compare against the alarm system it would replace. Where they differ, which is right?

## 5. What cbc can and cannot check

Stated together, because the split is the useful part.

**Mechanically checked:** mandatory `range` / `stale_after` / `release`; unreasoned sensor-health observations;
claims that reach no action; claims true in no reachable state; dead actions; ambiguity partitioned into genuine and
instrument-incoherence; at least one defeasible claim; the emitted fingerprint; and every invariant over every state.

**Not checkable, and stated as such:**

- whether a symptom is *damaging by itself* — structurally identical to any other claim
- whether **assumption** or **exception** was the right form — both are valid constructs
- whether the severity numbers are right
- whether the defeaters are the *complete* set of reasons a conclusion might fail

That last one is the real limit of the product, and no taxonomy removes it. It is why `cbc` refuses to accept a
defeater-free casebook, why the authoring guide exists, and why the strongest missing evidence is still someone outside
BioDream encoding a document quorum's developers have never seen.
